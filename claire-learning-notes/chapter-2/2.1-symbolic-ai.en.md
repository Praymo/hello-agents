# Even the Most Complete Rules Cannot Contain Reality: Understanding Early Agents Through Symbolic AI

[中文版](./2.1-symbolic-ai.md)

> Claire's personal learning notes on Section 2.1 of Datawhale's [Hello-Agents](https://github.com/datawhalechina/hello-agents). The short concept summary comes from the course. The reflections on relationships and game NPCs are my own, and do not represent the authors' views.

## The course concepts first

Symbolic AI tries to represent the world with symbols and reason with explicit rules. The Physical Symbol System Hypothesis (PSSH) made the question of whether symbolic operations could produce intelligent behavior into something researchers could try to build.

Expert systems encode domain knowledge as `IF–THEN` rules and use an inference engine to work from facts toward conclusions; MYCIN is one example. SHRDLU could understand commands, track state, and plan actions in a world of blocks. These systems show what symbolic methods can do when the rules and environment are describable. Outside a closed world, it becomes hard to write down all the knowledge, context, and exceptions.

## My reflections

### 1. Symbolic AI and judging relationships

While learning about early agents based on symbols and logic, I thought of how easily we slip into a kind of “symbolic” thinking when getting to know someone, especially when deciding what a relationship means.

Early agents tried to represent the world with symbols, handle problems through rules, and reason their way to conclusions. In love, we do something similar with `if-then` rules: if someone is steady, sincere, respects boundaries, and responds to me, then they must be right for me. If they are distant, avoidant, passive, or unable to express themselves, then they must not like me enough, or cannot be trusted.

We may even build a scoring system in our heads. We label a person's character, circumstances, behavior, and way of expressing themselves, then try to derive a definite answer from those labels. This way of judging is not useless. It can bring some clarity to confused feelings and help us spot obvious risks. But a person cannot be sorted into a simple binary category.

The same trait can mean very different things in different people. Silence might mean indifference or avoidance; it might also mean restraint or taking time to process feelings. Being forceful might reflect a need for control, or the ability to make a judgment and take responsibility when it matters. What matters, then, is not merely naming a trait. It is understanding what that trait does within this particular person's character.

### 2. The value and limits of a framework

**The value of symbolic AI** is that it makes intelligence clear, explainable, and possible to engineer. It breaks the vague and complicated idea of “intelligence” into symbols, rules, and reasoning steps, giving a machine an explicit path through a problem.

Likewise, we need some standards and frameworks in relationships. A framework can protect us, help us recognize risks, and screen out obvious mismatches. Without one, judgment can collapse into pure feeling, infatuation, or convincing ourselves of whatever we want to believe.

**But a framework has clear limits.** Symbolic systems work well when the rules are clear and the environment is closed. In an open, complicated world full of context, the rules cannot all be written down, common sense cannot be exhausted, and exceptions keep appearing. A relationship is an open-world problem too. In real life, a person's behavior rarely has a single-label explanation. It also depends on their experiences, character, ability to express themselves, current state, and place in the relationship.

A more mature judgment does not discard reason. It recognizes where a rational framework ends:

- People can be analyzed, but never fully reduced to a formula.
- Relationships can be evaluated, but never fully predicted.
- Attraction has reasons, but those reasons may never explain all of it.

A framework can help with initial screening and self-protection. It cannot replace spending real time with someone, or fully explain a living person.

### 3. You cannot write out the whole world—or the whole person

Symbolic AI is rather like trying to write the world as a complete system of rules: if the world can be represented clearly and the rules are rigorous enough, the system can follow them step by step, reach conclusions, and appear intelligent.

Its real difficulty, though, is not that the rules lack rigor. The real world simply cannot be written out in full.

It reminds me of NPCs in traditional story-driven games. An NPC might seem to have many responses, even hundreds of possible endings. Yet those endings are still paths written in advance by the developers. Whatever the player chooses, the system can only move through existing branches; it cannot grow a new story from its own experience.

A symbolic system can likewise look very smart because it has intricate rules built into it. But that intelligence still comes from a preset script.

To move beyond that limit, a system cannot depend solely on rules written ahead of time by people. It needs to learn patterns of language, knowledge, and behavior from large amounts of data, and respond dynamically in context. Models such as ChatGPT are still limited by training data, system instructions, and safety boundaries, but they do more than follow a fixed set of scripted paths.

So this is how I understand the section: the problem with symbolic systems is not a lack of logic. It is that they have only the logic **written into them**. Early AI faced the problem that **the world cannot be fully written down**. Perhaps the problem in relationships is that **a person cannot be fully written down either**.

Frameworks can help us understand the world and another person. But genuine understanding often happens beyond the framework.
